A short foreword · Three working hypotheses

Data Scientists in the Era of AI

Frontier labs' agents have rapidly changed how we work—but not necessarily how companies work. By “we,” I mainly mean big tech. These are three hypotheses about what has to happen before that change reaches everyone else. They are my opinions and working hypotheses, not settled conclusions. I love talking about where AI and data science are heading, and I hope these ideas spark a few thoughtful discussions.

01

Adoption starts with accessible data

Agents are highly successful where they can analyze vast codebases and work across well-connected systems, as they often can at technology companies. If a company's knowledge is scattered across Excel files, faxes—yes, they are still a thing—and Microsoft Access databases, the challenge becomes much greater. The companies facing those technical constraints may also have the hardest change-management work ahead of them.

02

Automation does not remove accountability

Even at technology companies, you still need someone to blame—or, more constructively, someone who is accountable. I do not imagine a world run only by agents: CEOs will keep their VPs, and teams at every level will still need people who own decisions and outcomes. What does seem clear is that entry-level roles are most exposed. Routine visualization and quick reporting are natural work for agents; advanced problems requiring judgment, context, and creativity remain safer—for now.

03

Connectors may matter more than models

An assistant that can reliably book an appointment or flight, query internal systems, and complete a workflow is already extremely useful; it does not need IQ-300 reasoning. For most companies, connecting databases and operational tools—and making their data trustworthy and available—may matter more to day-to-day work than another incremental leap in LLM intelligence or agent-harness sophistication.

About

Hi there and welcome. I started building this page to showcase projects I'd like to share — and also to learn some HTML. I try to explain my projects and the steps I take in detail, since I know how confusing it can be to look at random code snippets without any description.

It's been quite a journey for me, transitioning from a supply chain / business intelligence background into data science and analytics, so I hope this is helpful for other aspiring data scientists and analysts too. I work at the intersection of business, data, and technology: my business analytics program gave me the stats and coding skills that I've enhanced with business knowledge to solve complex business problems. Understanding the business problem is often the most challenging part, but it's crucial before starting any analysis — only with a clear goal in mind can analytics turn into insight, and insight into impact on strategy and operations.

My consulting background and the technical foundation I built in graduate school are especially useful in big tech, where data science questions often begin with something broad like, “How should we improve feature X?” Answering that question is rarely just analysis: it can mean finding and shaping the right data, measuring the feature's impact, identifying opportunities, estimating which improvements could deliver meaningful ROI, and presenting a clear recommendation. In that sense, product data science is full stack—from discovering where to invest and proposing solutions to measuring whether the implementation ultimately lived up to expectations.

Currently, I work as a data scientist in NYC after a longer stint in the Bay Area. I really enjoyed spending a lot of time outdoors exploring the city and nature out there — but weekly hiking has since changed to comedy clubs and Broadway shows. Still on the hunt for a local DS and tech community as tight-knit as the one I found in the Bay!

Cheers,
